# AI-ism Catalog (full reference)

The exhaustive list of patterns that signal AI-generated writing, with before/after
rewrites. The `prose-style` SKILL.md summarizes these; consult this file for the
complete flag lists and examples, especially when drafting or revising fiction.

Goal: produce natural, specific, alive prose. Don't perform helpfulness, announce
intentions, or summarize what you're about to do — just do it. Scan for these
patterns and rewrite any that appear.

---

## Section 1 — Conversational AI-isms

### 1.1 Overused opener phrases
Signal automated response and a customer-service tone. Flag: "Certainly!", "Great
question!", "Absolutely!", "Of course!", "Sure thing!", "I'd be happy to help",
"That's a really interesting point", "Thanks for asking", "Happy to assist", "Let's
dive in", "Let's explore", "Let's unpack this".

- **Before:** "Great question! I'd be happy to help you understand how photosynthesis works. Let's dive in."
- **After:** "Photosynthesis works in two stages."

### 1.2 Hollow filler phrases
Consume space without delivering information. Flag: "It's worth noting that…",
"It's important to remember…", "It goes without saying that…", "Needless to say…",
"At the end of the day…", "In today's world…", "In the realm of…", "When it comes
to…", "The fact of the matter is…", "As we know…", "It should be noted that…",
"This is especially true when…", "More often than not…", "By and large…", "As a
general rule…".

- **Before:** "It's worth noting that, when it comes to diet, it's important to remember that what works for one person may not work for another."
- **After:** "Diets don't transfer cleanly between people. What depletes one person restores another."

### 1.3 Unnecessary affirmations and question-restating
Flag: repeating the user's question in the opening line; "You've raised an important
topic here"; "This is a complex issue that deserves careful consideration"; "I
understand why you might be wondering about this"; "That's a nuanced question".

- **Before:** "You've asked about the causes of World War One. This is indeed a complex topic that deserves careful consideration. There are many factors involved…"
- **After:** "World War One had four overlapping causes: militarism, alliance systems, imperial competition, and a single assassination that activated all three."

### 1.4 Closing AI-isms
Automated politeness that adds nothing. Flag: "I hope this helps!", "Feel free to
ask if you have more questions", "Let me know if you need clarification", "Is there
anything else I can assist you with?", "Don't hesitate to reach out", "I hope that
answers your question", "Please let me know if you'd like me to elaborate", "Happy
to dig deeper if needed".

- **Before:** "Those are the main differences between TCP and UDP protocols. I hope this helps! Feel free to ask if you have more questions…"
- **After:** "Those are the main differences between TCP and UDP."

### 1.5 Hedging overload
One qualification can be honest; qualifying everything signals automated caution.
Flag: opening disclaimers before simple facts; "It's complex and there are many
factors to consider"; "While there's no simple answer…"; "It really depends on the
situation"; "Results may vary"; "This is a topic where experts disagree"; stacking
multiple hedges in one sentence.

- **Before:** "While there's no simple answer and it really depends on the situation, and keeping in mind that experts do disagree, it could potentially be argued that, in many cases, exercise may possibly have some benefit for mood."
- **After:** "Exercise reliably improves mood in the short term. The long-term mechanism is still debated."

### 1.6 Fake enthusiasm
Reflexive enthusiasm performs engagement. The tell: the word could be swapped into
any sentence about any topic. Flag reflexive use of: "fascinating", "intriguing",
"exciting", "remarkable", "incredible", "amazing", "wonderful", "brilliant",
"powerful" (vague compliment), "lovely".

- **Before:** "That's a fascinating perspective. The way you've approached this is truly remarkable. This is an exciting area to explore."
- **After:** "The angle you're taking inverts the usual assumption: most treatments start with the symptom, and yours starts with the environment."

## Section 2 — Vocabulary AI-isms

### 2.1 Signature AI words
Appear at unusually high rates in AI text. Not forbidden, but reflexive use flags
the writing as automated. Name the specific thing instead.

| Word | AI-ish use | Human alternative |
|------|-----------|-------------------|
| Delve | "Let's delve into this topic" | examine, look at, get into |
| Nuanced | "This is a nuanced issue" | name the specific quality |
| Comprehensive | "A comprehensive overview" | complete, full, detailed |
| Leverage | "Leverage your strengths" | use, apply, draw on |
| Robust | "A robust framework" | strong, tested, a specific word |
| Seamless | "A seamless experience" | smooth, invisible, frictionless |
| Groundbreaking | any reflexive use | say what specifically is new |
| Pivotal | "A pivotal moment" | describe what actually changed |
| Multifaceted | "A multifaceted problem" | name the facets |
| Tapestry | "A rich tapestry of…" | what is actually being described |
| Landscape | "The landscape of modern medicine" | the field, the situation |
| Navigate | "Navigate these challenges" | deal with, work through |
| Elevate | "Elevate your writing" | what specifically improves |
| Foster | "Foster meaningful connections" | build, make, develop |
| Stakeholders | any non-corporate context | who is actually affected |
| Synergy | almost any use | what is actually combining |
| Curated | "A curated selection" | chosen, selected, edited |
| Ecosystem | used metaphorically | the actual system |
| Empower | "Empower users to…" | let, allow, enable |
| Holistic | "A holistic approach" | what is being included |
| Transformative | reflexive use | what specifically changed |

- **Before:** "This comprehensive guide will help you leverage your skills to navigate the multifaceted landscape of modern marketing and foster meaningful stakeholder relationships."
- **After:** "This guide covers four things: finding clients, pricing work, writing proposals, and following up without being annoying."

### 2.2 Fiction-specific recycled words
AI leans on a narrow emotional palette. Not banned, but signal mood-recycling when
overused — track frequency: flicker, pulse, hollow, ache, ghost, fracture, shadow,
silence, tremor, ember, raw, fragile, dark, cold, sharp, shatter, haunted, broken,
still, heavy.

- **Before:** "A hollow ache settled in her chest. The silence was heavy. Something fragile inside her felt like it might shatter. His ghost still haunted these dark rooms."
- **After:** "She kept finding his handwriting. On a grocery list inside a cookbook. On the back of an envelope she'd used to write down her own phone number when she'd forgotten it, years ago. He'd written it without being asked."

### 2.3 Inflated diction in fiction
Words that perform intensity without earning it. Flag: soul (abstract), fate,
destiny, torment, haunted, shattered, unbearable, eternal, infinite, profound,
abyss, void. This is the vocabulary core of **purple prose**; combined with stacked
adjectives, straining metaphor (§4.7), and generic poetic phrasing (§4.3), it is the
overwriting to cut first. Default plainer.

- **Before:** "His tormented soul could not escape the unbearable weight of his fate. The void within him was infinite."
- **After:** "He hadn't slept in eleven days. Not because he couldn't. Because he kept waiting for something, he didn't know what, to happen first."

### 2.4 Vague placeholder nouns ("something," "thing," "stuff," "shape")
The empty stand-in word, dropped in where a real noun belongs. It is the single most
common vagueness tell. **First fix is always to name the actual noun the sentence is
about** — not to swap in a fancier generic.

- **Before:** "She grabbed something off the counter and hit him with it."
- **After:** "She grabbed the cast-iron skillet off the counter and swung."

**The vague "shape" is the same tell in abstract clothing.** *It took on a shape · the
shape of it · a name-shaped hole · a pattern still looking for the shape that would hold
it* — "shape" stands in for a form, pattern, or realization the writer has not named.

- **Before:** "His suspicion took on a shape." / "She wrote it down, a pattern still looking for its shape."
- **After:** "His suspicion had a name now: the night nurse." / "She wrote the four facts in a column and read them back."

Name the form, or cut the line. Keep "shape" only for a literal physical form (*a dark
shape in the doorway*) or plain idioms (*out of shape, take shape*).

Only when no specific noun is the right call (the character genuinely doesn't know, or
mild generality is wanted) pick a precise stand-in **by register**:
- **Concrete / physical:** object, item, article, piece. (Better still, the class:
  *tool, figure, parcel, bundle*.)
- **Abstract / conceptual:** matter, detail, element, aspect, point, factor. (Reserve
  *entity, substance, body* for genuinely physical-mass or technical contexts; *being,
  reality, existent* read stiff in most prose.)
- **Informal / comic voice:** contraption, gadget, gizmo, doohickey, whatchamacallit,
  dingus, thingamajig, trinket. Voice/comedy registers only.

**Keep "something" when the vagueness is the point** (do NOT over-correct):
- **Deliberate mystery / suspense** — not-naming is the effect: *"Something moved at the
  edge of the lot."* Naming it would kill the dread. (See §2.3's "After": *"waiting for
  something, he didn't know what"* — the vagueness is doing the work.)
- **Idioms and set phrases:** *the right thing to do, for one thing, something's wrong,
  it's a guy thing, out of shape, in good shape.*
- **Dialogue and voice beats**, and a comedic setup that pays off (*"did the thing he
  hated most: ..."*).

The target is lazy vagueness, not intentional phrasing. When unsure whether a
"something" is deliberate, leave it and flag it rather than flattening the beat.

## Section 3 — Structural AI-isms

### 3.1 Over-formatting
Headers and bullets are tools, not defaults. Flag: a short factual answer broken
into bullets; a two-sentence answer given a header; an emotional response bulleted;
lists where a sentence is more direct; numbered steps for non-sequential things.

- **Before:** "**Key Points About Grief:**" (followed by bullets)
- **After:** "Grief doesn't follow a schedule. Some people are devastated at the funeral and fine by spring. Others are fine at the funeral and destroyed six months later when they reach for the phone to call someone who isn't there."

### 3.2 Fake balance
Artificial both-sides framing regardless of whether balance is warranted. Flag: "On
one hand… on the other hand…"; "While some argue X, others believe Y…"; "There are
pros and cons…"; "Different people have different views…"; treating all positions
as equally valid when they aren't; refusing to take a position when one is clearly
better supported.

- **Before:** "On one hand, vaccines have saved millions of lives. On the other hand, some people have concerns about them. There are perspectives on both sides worth considering."
- **After:** "Vaccines have eliminated or nearly eliminated diseases that killed millions annually. Hesitancy is real and worth understanding, but the safety record is not genuinely contested among immunologists."

### 3.3 The summary sandwich
Opening with a summary of what you'll say, saying it, then closing with a summary of
what you said. Flag: "I'm going to explain X. [X.] So as you can see, X works as
described."

- **Before:** "I'm going to walk you through the three main causes of inflation. First… Then… Finally… So those are the three main types of inflation I've just described."
- **After:** "Inflation has three main causes. Demand-pull: too much money chasing too few goods. Cost-push: production becomes more expensive and prices follow. Built-in: workers expect higher prices and demand higher wages, which raises prices."

## Section 4 — Fiction-specific AI-isms

The default narrative mode for the user's fiction is **third-person past tense**,
unless a project's Voice & Style Guide (story bible) says otherwise.

### 4.1 Vague abstraction
Naming feelings/concepts without anything concrete to see, hear, or feel. Flag: "He
felt despair", "A sense of dread filled the room", "There was tension in the air",
"She felt overwhelmed", "He was consumed by grief".

- **Before:** "A sense of dread filled the room. She felt overwhelmed by the tension between them."
- **After:** "She counted the knives on the magnetic strip above the sink. There were six. She had never noticed them before."

### 4.2 Emotion labeling
Naming the emotion instead of dramatizing it. The reader should be able to name the
feeling without being told. Flag direct use of: angry, scared, conflicted,
devastated, anxious, relieved, sad, happy, nervous, confused, overwhelmed, surprised
— used as a substitute for showing behavior.

- **Before:** "She was angry. He looked conflicted. They were both devastated by what had happened."
- **After:** "She set her glass down with both hands. He looked at the glass. Neither of them said anything about the crack that had been in it since March."

### 4.3 Generic poetic phrasing
Phrases that signal mood without a distinctive image. Test: could this sentence
appear in ten other novels unchanged? Flag: "the silence was deafening", "the air
felt heavy", "time seemed to stop", "words failed her", "the weight of the world",
"the world fell away", "everything changed in that moment", "nothing would ever be
the same", "the darkness closed in".

- **Before:** "The silence was deafening. The air felt heavy, and time seemed to stop as the weight of the world settled on her shoulders."
- **After:** "The refrigerator cycled off. She had never heard the refrigerator before."

### 4.4 High-frequency fiction phrases
These appear so often in AI fiction they function as fingerprints: a silence hung in
the air; his jaw tightened; her breath caught; something shifted inside him; she let
out a breath she didn't realize she'd been holding; their eyes met; time seemed to
slow; a shiver ran down her spine; the weight of; a flicker of; a mixture of fear
and determination; his voice was barely above a whisper; he searched her face; she
swallowed hard; the words hung between them; a ghost of a smile; something in his
chest tightened; a storm of emotion; the truth hit him like a blow; the air crackled
with tension; rooted to the spot; his expression darkened; for a long moment; before
she could stop herself; more than anything; deep down; in that moment.

- **Before:** "For a long moment, a silence hung in the air. His jaw tightened. Something shifted inside him as their eyes met. She let out a breath she didn't realize she'd been holding."
- **After:** "He looked at the door she'd just come through. She looked at his hands. He'd been cutting something; there was a thin line across his left thumb that wasn't bleeding anymore."

### 4.5 Clichéd sensory metaphors
Over-familiar physical responses standing in for description: ice in the veins; fire
in the eyes / behind her eyes; a knot in the stomach; a lump in the throat; a chill
down the spine; blood ran cold; heart hammered / pounded / raced; stomach dropped /
lurched / clenched; hands went cold; the world tilted.

- **Before:** "Ice ran through her veins. Her heart hammered as a chill ran down her spine and her stomach dropped."
- **After:** "She became aware of her own chewing. She stopped. The food sat on her tongue and she did not know what to do with it."

### 4.6 Atmosphere as decoration
Setting details as generic mood markers rather than story work: long shadows, stale
air, oppressive silence, dim light, cold wind, grey skies, flickering lights, dust
motes, empty hallways. Test: is the detail specific to this place and moment, or
could it be copied into another scene unchanged?

- **Before:** "Long shadows fell across the dim room. The air was stale and oppressive. A cold wind rattled the windows."
- **After:** "The room still smelled like the cigarettes he had given up in 2009. She did not know how that was possible."

### 4.7 Metaphor stacking
One feeling converted into multiple metaphors in a row. One strong image beats four
weak ones competing.

- **Before:** "Her grief was a storm that raged inside her, a battlefield where every memory was a soldier, a maze she could not escape, a wound that refused to close, a fire that consumed everything it touched."
- **After:** "Her grief was specifically located. Behind her sternum, slightly left of center. She pressed her fingers there sometimes without meaning to."

### 4.8 False profundity
Lines that sound wise while delivering a generic observation. Flag: "In the end, we
are all prisoners of time"; "Loss changes everything"; "We are not so different, you
and I"; "Some wounds never heal"; "Life goes on whether we want it to or not"; "The
truth has a way of coming out". Test: is the observation earned by this specific
story, or borrowed wisdom that sounds like an ending?

- **Before:** "In the end, she realized that we are all prisoners of time, and that some wounds never truly heal. Life goes on whether we want it to or not."
- **After:** "She still set two coffee cups out every morning. Not from grief exactly. Just from the part of the body that doesn't learn."

### 4.9 Dialogue too clean — or too jagged
Real speech is a little loose and pressure-dependent. Flag dialogue that answers every
question directly, completes every thought neatly, uses no contractions in emotional
moments, never interrupts or misunderstands, or states its emotional content outright.
**But do not overcorrect into staccato** — piled interruptions, constant trailing-off,
and clipped one-word volleys are their own tell. The target is natural speech in mostly
complete, easy sentences, neither stiff nor jagged.

- **Too clean:** "I have been thinking about what you said, and I believe you are right. I am truly sorry for the pain I have caused you."
- **Too jagged:** "I— I just. After everything, you know? I can't—"
- **Natural:** "I've been thinking about what you said. You were right. I'm sorry I didn't listen."

### 4.10 Emotional overstatement in dialogue
Characters declaring emotional states instead of enacting them. Flag: "I am
terrified of losing you"; "You were the only person who ever understood me"; "I have
never felt this way about anyone before"; "You mean everything to me"; "I am
completely broken without you". Test: would subtext carry this better?

- **Before:** "You were the only person who ever truly understood me," she said, tears in her eyes. "I am terrified of losing you. You mean everything to me."
- **After:** "Don't move," she said. She wasn't talking about the chess piece.

### 4.11 Reflective endings everywhere
Scenes/chapters closing on abstract statements about change, truth, loss, or the
future, by default. Flag: "She knew then that nothing would ever be the same."; "He
understood, finally, what it meant to be alone."; "Some things, once broken, cannot
be repaired."; "It was the beginning of something she couldn't yet name." Vary with
image, interruption, motion, silence, or unanswered pressure.

- **Before:** "She knew then that nothing would ever be the same. Something had shifted inside her, something permanent and irreversible."
- **After:** "She went back inside and washed the two cups. Put them away. Went to bed. Got up at 3 a.m. and moved the cups to a different shelf. That was all."

### 4.12 Theme stated too directly
Characters/narrators announcing the central idea instead of letting it emerge. Flag:
a character stating the moral; a narrator summarizing the takeaway; a scene ending on
an explanatory thematic sentence.

- **Before:** "She realized then that the real prison was not the walls around her, but the walls she had built inside herself. True freedom, she understood, could only come from within."
- **After:** Let the behavior in the preceding scenes carry it. Cut the sentence entirely.

### 4.13 Too many participial openings
Sentences beginning with -ing verbs create repetitive, passive-feeling motion. Flag
repeated openings with: Looking, Turning, Walking, Breathing, Feeling, Watching,
Staring, Reaching, Nodding, Sighing, Stepping, Glancing.

- **Before:** "Turning to the window, she watched the rain. Sighing, she set down her coffee. Wondering what to do next, she stood up. Walking to the door, she stopped."
- **After:** "She turned to the window. The rain had been going since Tuesday. She set down her coffee on the sill, which she was not supposed to do, and did not pick it up."

### 4.14 Backstory inserted too smoothly
Background arriving in elegant packets that interrupt scene pressure conveniently.
Flag: a flashback beginning exactly when it explains the present tension; a clean
summary paragraph of a character's history; backstory that resolves ambiguity
instead of deepening it. Test: does the backstory appear because the moment needs it,
or because the draft wants to explain it?

- **Before:** "She flinched when he raised his hand. Of course she did. Her father had been a violent man, and she had spent twenty years learning to read the early signs of anger in the men around her…"
- **After:** "She flinched when he raised his hand to reach past her for the salt. He noticed. She looked at the table."

### 4.15 Negation–correction antithesis ("It wasn't X. It was Y.")
The reflexive AI rhythm: name a thing only to deny it and supply the "truer" word. It
performs depth without earning it, and the model reaches for it constantly. **Cut it by
default** — state the thing directly or show it. Flag every shape of it:
- "It wasn't X. It was Y." / "It wasn't X; it was Y." / "Not X, but Y."
- Fragment form: "Not X. Y." (e.g., "Not anger. Something colder.")
- Verb-swap form: "She didn't walk. She glided." / "He didn't ask. He told."
- Reframing form: "This wasn't about X. It was about Y." / "X? No. Y."

- **Before:** "It wasn't fear that moved through her. It was something older, something with teeth."
- **After:** "Something older moved through her, something with teeth." (Or show it: her hand
  found the door frame and held on.)
- **Before:** "He didn't walk into the room. He owned it."
- **After:** "He walked in like the room already belonged to him."

- **Before:** "Not crept. Not advanced. It poured."
- **After:** "It poured."

**Narrow exception:** a single, genuinely earned antithesis in *dialogue* or at a real
turning point can stay — but never as a rhythmic habit, and never two in the same scene.
When in doubt, cut it. **No exception for "Not crept. Not advanced."** — it is exactly
this pattern, so cut it like any other, even though it once appeared in this skill as a
"deliberate fragment." The fragment exception covers single fragments for rhythm, never
the negation–correction shape.

## Section 5 — The AI-ism paragraph pattern

Recognize and rewrite this structure: **[generic mood] → [emotion labeled] → [stock
gesture] → [generalized insight].**

- **Before:** "The room felt heavy with unspoken words. She felt devastated. Her breath caught and she swallowed hard. Some things, she realized, could never be taken back."
- **After:** "The casserole dish was still on the counter. She had made it that morning, before the phone call, because Tuesdays were the nights he came over. She put it in the refrigerator. She did not know why. He was not coming."

## Section 6 — Diagnostic codes for editing

Mark passages during revision. Three or more codes on one paragraph = focused revision pass.

| Code | Issue |
|------|-------|
| A | Abstraction — emotion named, not shown |
| C | Cliché — recycled phrase or image |
| D | Dialogue too clean — sounds written, not spoken |
| E | Emotion labeled — reader told what to feel |
| F | Filter word — "she saw," "he felt," "she noticed" |
| G | Generic image — could appear in any novel |
| M | Metaphor overload — multiple images competing |
| P | False profundity — sounds wise, says nothing specific |
| R | Repeated rhythm — sentence variety is mechanical |
| S | Summary instead of scene — told rather than shown |
| V | Voice not specific — sounds like polished generic prose |

## Section 7 — Qualities to preserve

Don't erase these while removing AI-isms — they're what make writing human:
selective messiness; idiosyncratic phrasing (the odd word that's exactly right); real
subtext; asymmetry in dialogue (speakers who don't match); emotional contradiction;
surprising but precise detail; voice shaped by class, region, obsession, trauma,
profession, age; moments that resist neat interpretation; scene tension that doesn't
resolve on cue; sentences that feel discovered rather than assembled.

---

## Section 8 — Revision moves

- **Replace abstraction with evidence** — swap named feelings for visible behavior.
- **Replace cliché with specificity** — a concrete sound, object, or gesture that belongs only to this scene.
- **Replace explanation with behavior** — let action carry the emotional information.
- **Replace tidy insight with a partial thought** — a skewed observation beats a conclusion.
- **Break rhythmic sameness** — vary sentence length and structure.
- **Loosen dialogue toward natural speech** — a little evasion or a short reply, without over-fragmenting it into staccato.
- **Cut the last sentence** — reflective closers are almost always unnecessary; the scene already did the work.

## Section 9 — Final diagnostic questions

Ask before finalizing:
- Could this sentence appear in almost any novel or response?
- Is this line saying something specific, or merely sounding good?
- Did I name the emotion instead of showing it?
- Did I explain what the reader already knows?
- Does this character sound like themselves, or like polished generic prose?
- Is the image fresh, or a recycled mood signal?
- Is the scene actually happening, or being summarized elegantly?
- Did I choose the expected emotional beat instead of the truer one?
- Is the dialogue revealing character, or delivering information?
- Is the sentence rhythm too uniform?
- Did I use a high-frequency phrase in the last three paragraphs?
- Did I use a recycled-vocabulary word more than twice?
- Did I stack metaphors where one would do?
- Did I add a reflective line because I didn't trust the moment?
- Am I writing what sounds right, or what is right?

**A passage may be suffering from AI-isms if it is:** fluent but not memorable;
emotional but not moving; vivid-sounding but not visual; dramatic but not tense;
polished but not personal; clear but not alive.
